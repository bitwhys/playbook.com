This illustrative sample explores a simple question: why do small samples swing
so much? It uses a hypothetical fair coin, not observed research data.

## The same coin, different sample sizes

Imagine repeating an experiment: flip a fair coin a fixed number of times, then
record the proportion of heads. Even with the same coin, that proportion varies
between experiments.

The **standard error** describes the standard deviation of those proportions.
Increase the number of flips below to see how it changes.

## The calculation

For independent flips with probability `p` of heads and sample size `n`, the
standard error is the square root of `p * (1 - p) / n`.

```ts standard-error.ts
const probability = 0.5
const sampleSize = 100
const standardError = Math.sqrt((probability * (1 - probability)) / sampleSize)
// 0.05, or 5 percentage points
```

There are two useful consequences:

- More flips reduce the variability of the proportion of heads.
- Four times as many flips halve the standard error.

> A larger sample improves precision under this model. It does not fix a biased
> measurement process or make dependent observations independent.

## Try it yourself

1. Start at 100 flips: the standard error is 5 percentage points.
2. Increase to 400 flips: it falls to 2.5 percentage points.
3. Return to 10 flips and notice how much wider the variation can be.
